Exhibit 01 // The market

The demand already exists. It just can't reach a wave.

The Pacific Northwest isn't a blank market that needs creating — it's an under-served one. Surfers are here in volume; the supply is hours away, cold, dangerous, and seasonal. That gap is the opportunity.

The nearest planned wave is outdoors, seasonal — and stuck.

A Wavegarden surf park near Yakima ("Barreled") was approved and water-secured. It is ~2.5 hours from Seattle, unheated, and open only April–October — and it has been unable to raise its ~$30–40M for years.

  • An in-city, year-round, heated wave competes against nothing that exists today.
  • The captive base doesn't have to be created — it has to be given a closer option.
  • Yakima's stall is the clearest read on the local appetite for big outdoor capital — and the reason to enter small.

Exhibit 03 // The technology

A mature, de-risked solution space.

This is not a science project. Every wave system below is commercially deployed. The first decision is which side of one fork you're on — and it determines everything about cost and whether you can be indoors.

Traveling-wave lagoons

Ocean feel · 2–5+ acres · hard to enclose
  • Wave system alone runs ~$13M; total projects $30M → $78M+
  • You paddle in and ride down the line, like the sea
  • Enclosing acres of water = stadium-scale construction
Right for destinations and real-estate anchors — wrong for a lean indoor entry.

Standing waves

Compact · fits in a building · year-round
  • Wave system ~$1–10M — an order of magnitude cheaper
  • A continuous wave you ride in place — built for repetition
  • Purpose-made for indoor, urban, year-round operation
The realistic indoor play, and ideal for an "academy" built on coaching and progression.

The call // Recommendation

Land and expand: prove it small, then build it big.

For a skeptical investor, the right move is the one that risks the least to answer the biggest question — "will Seattle pay to surf?" — and keeps every upside option open. That is a two-step.

Step 1 · prove demandPath A

An urban standing-wave academy

UNIT-anchored · ~$1.5M wave · a Seattle warehouse

The smallest check that tests the thesis. A compact, heated, year-round wave inside an ordinary building, run as a coaching academy: lessons, memberships, youth camps, leagues, corporate bookings — recurring revenue that throws off cash and, crucially, real Seattle demand data.

Standing-wave tech is cheap, indoor by design, low-water, and ideal for repetition. The same UNIT is modular, expandable, and even relocatable — so the asset is never stranded.

→
Step 2 · capture the upsidePath D

A surf-anchored development

Wave as amenity · funded by land & home sales

Once the academy is full and profitable, you hold the one thing the stalled Yakima project never had: proven local numbers. That de-risks the larger raise — and the outsized returns live here, in land appreciation, not session tickets.

It's the model that actually gets financed: Utah's Southern Shores uses the very same UNIT wave to sell premium lots. You'd bring the operating track record; a developer partner carries the land risk.

Smallest check, biggest question

~$ millions, not tens of millions, to answer "is there a Seattle market?" — the only question that matters first.

Recurring & fad-resistant

An academy of lessons, memberships and camps is the least novelty-exposed revenue in this industry.

No stranded asset

The UNIT carries into Path D — modular, expandable, even movable. Step 1's hardware becomes Step 2's amenity.

Contained downside, open upside

If demand underwhelms, losses are bounded. If it's strong, the real-estate option is right there, now financeable.

What not to lead with

Do not open with Path C (an enclosed earthen lake): it pours the most capital into the most uncertain cost — humidity, heating and the clear-span — before demand is proven. And do not open with a standalone outdoor lagoon: it's seasonal in this climate, and the Yakima stall shows that big outdoor capital can't currently raise here. Both become rational after Step 1, not before.

Exhibit 05 // What could go wrong

The honest risk register.

A thesis that hides its risks isn't worth funding. Here are the real ones — and why the land-and-expand structure is specifically designed to absorb them.

Risk 01 · Track record

Surf parks have failed before.

NLand in Texas opened in 2016 and closed in 2018. The sector has real casualties.

MitigantThose were giant first-generation lagoon bets. Step 1 is a small, standing-wave, recurring-revenue academy — the opposite risk profile.
Risk 02 · Operating cost

Seattle labor and energy aren't cheap.

Staff is the biggest line item, and energy scales with hours the wave runs.

MitigantA standing wave uses far less water and space; WA power is below the national average; memberships smooth revenue and demand-throttling cuts off-peak energy.
Risk 03 · Financing climate

Big surf capital can't raise here right now.

The approved Yakima park has been stuck on its ~$30–40M for years.

MitigantPrecisely why the entry is a few-million-dollar check, not tens of millions — and why the big raise comes only after demand is proven.
Risk 04 · Novelty fade

Will it still be busy in year three?

Attractions can spike then cool. Pay-per-visit alone is fragile.

MitigantThe academy model is built on progression: tiered coaching, kids' programs, leagues, corporate and private events — recurring, not one-time.
Risk 05 · Site & permitting

Water and land approvals are slow.

Anything in public water (e.g. Lake Washington) faces a brutal multi-agency stack.

MitigantAn indoor warehouse avoids water permitting entirely; UNIT is fish-friendly certified and fully removable if ever sited on water.
Risk 06 · Execution

These are complex operating businesses.

Even Lost Shore had early food-and-service growing pains.

MitigantStarting small is the point: nail operations, staffing and unit economics at academy scale before any capital goes into a destination.
The thesis // Where this goes next

The bet is small. The market is real. The upside compounds.

Seattle holds a large, captive, affluent surfer base with no year-round option, in a climate that has proven demand everywhere it's been tried — and with two structural cost advantages, cheap hydro power and water abundance, that the desert and coastal competition can't match.

  1. Validate the unit economicsBuild the Path A model: riders/hour × pricing against a Seattle warehouse lease, labor and the UNIT's energy draw, with the academy + membership revenue stack layered in.
  2. Spec the anchor waveEngage UNIT Surf Pool for a Seattle-specific quote, real per-hour energy numbers, and an indoor warehouse footprint and power-service requirement.
  3. Lock a site & talk to the utilityScout clear-span flex space with the ceiling height and floor loading for the basin and pumps; open an early conversation with Seattle City Light on service capacity.
  4. Pressure-test demandPre-sell founding memberships and corporate packages to convert the 220,000-surfer base from a statistic into signed intent before opening.
  5. Pre-wire the Path D upsideBegin relationships with a residential developer so the scale-up is ready the moment academy numbers justify it.

The one-line thesis

Enter with the cheapest real wave in the country, prove Seattle demand with recurring revenue, then convert proven numbers into a surf-anchored real-estate play where the outsized returns live.
